### `ms-death-of-a-spouse-later-life` — Outliving a husband or wife

- **Lane:** People & family · **Kind:** A common pattern · optional
- **Renders as:** *not yet sourced* (no figure shown)
- **Care note:** “This names a common later-life event rather than forecasting anything for anyone, and the pages linked here are about what actually helps afterward.”
- **Routes:** /situations/a-death · /situations/grief
- **Authoring note:** Previously carried a span built from survey age universes: the lower edge of a Census denominator and the edges of American Community Survey age groups, with an open-ended oldest band truncated to make an upper bound. No source read in this build states an age at which this happens, so the record states its shape in words and carries no digits.
- **Timing analysis** (this is where tone risk is highest):
    - **early** — When a husband or wife dies earlier in adult life, the household is more often still carrying a mortgage, still raising children, and still built on two earnings. Several of the survivor routes are structured by age, so a younger surviving spouse reaches some of them through a child in the household rather than through their own age.
        - cost: Household income can drop while a mortgage, child care, and paid work all continue unchanged.
        - route: Social Security pays a surviving spouse who is caring for the couple's young child whatever the survivor's own age, and pays a survivor benefit on the survivor's own age from a set age onward; both are claimed through the Social Security Administration.
        - route: Life insurance proceeds, employer benefits, and pension survivor elections are claimed through the employer's benefits office, and each has its own filing window.
        - route: Legal aid organizations and probate court self-help centers handle estate paperwork for people without an attorney.
    - **window** — Outliving a husband or wife is a common later-life event, though no source read in this build prints an age at which it happens. What follows is much the same whenever it arrives: a household of two becomes a household of one, benefits and titles have to be redirected, and the tasks the other person handled land on one person at once.
    - **late** — At the oldest ages, a spouse's death tends to arrive when the surviving person has care needs of their own and a thinner circle of friends of the same age, so living alone becomes the common arrangement and housing decisions often follow within the first year. Driving, medications, bills, and the house are the practical things that change hands.
        - cost: Two Social Security payments become one, since a surviving spouse receives the higher of the two amounts rather than both, while housing costs stay where they were.
        - route: The Eldercare Locator and Area Agencies on Aging arrange transportation, meals, and in-home help, and can be called before anything is decided about housing.
        - route: Grief support groups run through hospice organizations, hospitals, and community and faith organizations, and most are open to anyone rather than only to the families of former patients.
        - route: The Social Security Administration handles the benefit change by phone or in an office, and the funeral home usually reports the death to it directly.
    - **interrupted** — What follows a spouse's death often stalls rather than proceeds. An estate sits in probate for months, accounts are frozen because a title was in one name only, or a couple was separated but not divorced, which changes who inherits and who may claim. None of this is unusual, and none of it is fixed by acting faster.
        - cost: Money that is legally the survivor's can be out of reach for months while an estate is open.
        - route: Small-estate affidavits and simplified probate exist in every state and clear modest estates without a full proceeding.
        - route: Accounts with a named beneficiary, and homes held with a transfer-on-death deed or in joint tenancy, pass outside probate entirely and are claimed with the provider or the recorder's office directly.
        - route: State unclaimed property offices hold accounts nobody claimed, and searching them costs nothing.
    - **alternative** — Long-term partners who never married reach the same event with none of the automatic standing. Survivor benefits, inheritance without a will, and next-of-kin authority follow a marriage or a document, not the length of a relationship, so a surviving partner's position rests entirely on what was written down beforehand.
        - cost: Without a will or a beneficiary designation, an unmarried partner inherits nothing under state intestacy rules, which pass an estate to blood relatives.
        - route: A will, beneficiary designations, a transfer-on-death deed, and joint ownership with right of survivorship each direct specific assets to a partner, and any of them can be put in place at any point.
        - route: A health care proxy and a durable power of attorney operate while both people are living; a will, beneficiary designations and a transfer-on-death deed are what carry effect afterward.
    - **never** — Outliving a spouse is not a stage anyone passes through; it happens in some households and not in others. People reach the oldest ages never having been widowed for many different reasons: they did not marry, a marriage ended by divorce, they partnered without a license, or theirs was the death that came first. The paperwork that makes any of these easier for whoever remains is the same in every case.
        - route: Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance pass outside a will and are updated in minutes with the account provider.
        - route: A will, a durable power of attorney, and a health care proxy are the standard set, and state court self-help centers publish forms for all three.
- **Not yet sourced.** Need a source that prints an age at which people are widowed, rather than the share of a fixed age band that is currently widowed. Try the Health and Retirement Study for transitions into widowhood by age, or a Census Survey of Income and Program Participation report giving a median age at widowhood, and look for one that reports women and men separately, since every marital-status tabulation found in this build shows a large divergence between them. The American Community Survey indicator and the Census story read in this build both tabulate marital status inside age groups only.

### `ms-first-birth-mean-age` — Becoming a parent for the first time

- **Lane:** People & family · **Kind:** A common pattern · optional
- **Renders as:** `commonly around 27.5`
- **Population:** Mothers recorded on United States birth certificates; the figure is the national mean age at first birth for the reference year, an average rather than a range, and the report gives separate averages for each level of urbanicity.
- **Care note:** “This is an average measured across a whole population rather than a schedule for any one person, and a clinician is the right place for a question about a particular body.”
- **Routes:** /topics/health
- **What changes:**
    - Sleep, daily time, and household spending reorganize around an infant, and one or both parents commonly change their working hours.
    - The average has moved later over recent years, and the report gives a higher average in large metropolitan counties than in the least urban ones.
    - Fathers are not counted in this measure, which is drawn from the mother's age on the birth record.
- **Authoring note:** The urbanicity figures are a variation note in the drawer, not an outer extent. They are the averages of two groups of counties, and the report itself shows first births at both younger and older ages than either of them.
- **Timing analysis** (this is where tone risk is highest):
    - **early** — A first birth earlier in adult life tends to arrive while schooling or a first stretch of paid work is still under way, so care and hours get arranged around an income that is usually still climbing. Parents who start earlier are commonly younger when their children reach adulthood, and their own parents are more often nearby and able to help with daily care.
        - cost: Child care lands in the years when earnings are typically lowest, so it takes a larger share of what a household brings in.
        - cost: Schooling or training in progress often has to be rearranged around care.
        - route: State child care subsidy agencies, Head Start, and Early Head Start are means-tested programs built for exactly these years.
        - route: Campus child care centers, part-time and evening enrollment, and stop-out-and-return policies are standard at community colleges and many public universities.
        - route: Family and Medical Leave Act job protection and state paid-family-leave programs cover some workers; eligibility turns on employer size and tenure.
    - **window** — Around the national average, a first birth commonly follows a stretch of paid work and often a partnership, so care and hours are arranged against a known income. The report gives a higher average in large metropolitan counties than in the least urban ones, which tracks local housing costs and the local mix of work rather than anything about individual bodies.
    - **late** — A first birth later in adult life tends to arrive with income and housing already settled, and with the new parent's own parents older and less often available for daily help. Across a population, conception takes longer on average as the reproductive years go on — the professional guidance describes a gradual change rather than a single point — so a clinician is more often part of the ordinary route.
        - cost: Daily help from a grandparent generation is less often available, so paid or arranged care carries more of the load.
        - route: Reproductive endocrinologists and fertility clinics are a routine medical route rather than a last resort; some employer plans and some states' insurance mandates cover part of the cost.
        - route: Adoption and foster-to-adopt licensing carry no general upper age bar, and each agency publishes its own criteria.
        - route: Paid care, nanny shares, and after-school programs substitute for family help where family help is not close by.
    - **interrupted** — Paths toward a first child stop and restart for many reasons: a partnership ends, a course of treatment is stopped, an adoption match does not go through, or a pregnancy does not continue. What tends to follow is a stretch with no institutional marker in it, since none of these is recorded anywhere the way a birth is.
        - cost: Treatment cycles and adoption processes cost money and time, and neither is returned when a path stops.
        - route: Fertility clinics report their outcomes to a federal reporting program, and a second clinical opinion is an ordinary step rather than a complaint.
        - route: RESOLVE, the national infertility association, runs peer support groups across the country, and therapists who specialize in reproductive health are listed through professional directories.
        - route: Licensed adoption agencies and state foster care systems treat a match that does not go through as routine, and a family stays in the process rather than starting it over.
    - **alternative** — Becoming a parent takes forms other than a first birth on a birth certificate: adoption, fostering, step-parenting, kinship care, donor conception, surrogacy, and shared parenting arrangements. Each has its own legal route, and the parental standing each creates differs from state to state rather than following automatically from the caregiving itself.
        - cost: Legal parentage is not automatic in several of these routes and has to be established, which takes time and usually an attorney.
        - route: Second-parent adoption, stepparent adoption, adoption decrees, and parentage judgments are the standard instruments.
        - route: Family-law attorneys and state court self-help centers handle them, and state foster care agencies license kinship caregivers directly.
    - **never** — Many adults never have a child, by decision or by circumstance, and the household that results is an ordinary household rather than an unfinished one. Time, money, and housing are arranged around adult needs; care later in life is more often arranged through paid services, siblings, friends, nieces and nephews than through adult children, and households that expect that tend to write the arrangement down rather than assume it.
        - cost: Hospitals, benefit programs, and courts often reach for an adult child as next of kin or as a default decision-maker.
        - route: A health care proxy and a durable power of attorney put decisions with whoever the person actually names, and a will directs an estate anywhere.
        - route: Professional fiduciaries, geriatric care managers, and elder-law attorneys take on the roles adult children otherwise fill, and are engaged the way any professional is.
- **The sentences the figures came from:**
    - “Mean age at first birth increased over time and rose 0.9 year from 26.6 in 2016 to 27.5 in 2023.”
      — National Center for Health Statistics, https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nvsr/nvsr74/nvsr74-09.pdf (data 2023, published 2025, checked 2026-09-03)
      — what it measured: Mean age of mothers at first birth, computed directly from the frequency of births by age of mother in all United States birth records.
    - “In 2023, mothers in large metropolitan counties had the highest mean age at first birth (28.5), while those in noncore counties had the lowest (24.8).”
      — National Center for Health Statistics, https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nvsr/nvsr74/nvsr74-09.pdf (data 2023, published 2025, checked 2026-09-03)
      — what it measured: Mean age of mothers at first birth by urbanicity of county of residence, from all United States birth records. The report prints a group average for each level of urbanicity; it does not report the spread of maternal age itself.
